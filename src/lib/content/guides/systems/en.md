# Volleyball offensive systems

<Callout>

**In short**

- A system is named after how many hitters and setters a team uses: **5-1** means five hitters and one setter.
- **6-6:** everyone sets in turn, from the middle front. The simplest, for beginners.
- **4-2:** two setters; whichever is in the **front row** sets. Only two front-row attackers.
- **6-2:** two setters; whichever is in the **back row** sets. Always three front-row attackers.
- **5-1:** one setter in all six rotations. The most common system from club level up.

</Callout>

## How systems are named

The first number is how many players attack, the second how many set. The numbers do not have to add up to six: in a 6-2, the two setters attack too, when they are in the front row. Whatever the system, the rotation rules are the same; the system only decides who takes the second ball.

A useful question for any system: **how many front-row attackers do we have?** Three attackers spread the opponent's block; two make the block's job easier.

## 6-6: everyone sets

In a 6-6 there is no fixed setter. Whoever rotates into **zone 3** (middle front) sets that rotation; the two other front-row players attack.

- **Front-row attackers:** two.
- **Pros:** everyone learns every skill, nobody needs to switch, and the rotation is the line-up. Ideal for beginners, school teams and mixed recreational groups.
- **Cons:** the quality of the set changes every rotation, and only two attackers are available. Some variants let the player in zone 2 set instead, so check your group's habit.

## 4-2: front-row setter

Two setters stand opposite each other in the rotation (three places apart), so exactly one is always in the front row. **The front-row setter sets**, usually from right front or middle front, and the four hitters attack.

- **Front-row attackers:** two (the setter uses one of the three front-row spots).
- **Pros:** simple. The setter is already at the net, so there is no running from the back row, and serve receive is easy to organise. A good step up from 6-6.
- **Cons:** only two attackers, so the block can focus on them.

## 6-2: back-row setter

Again two setters opposite each other, but now **the back-row setter sets**. The front-row setter plays as an attacker, usually on the right.

- **Front-row attackers:** always three.
- **Pros:** three attackers in every rotation, and two players share the setting load. Works well when a team has two decent setters who can also hit.
- **Cons:** the setter always comes from the back row, so serve receive needs more hiding (see [the overlap rule](/guides/overlap)). The two setters must deliver a similar ball, and there are more substitutions if the coach brings in specialist hitters for the setters.

## 5-1: one setter

One setter sets in every rotation. Opposite the setter in the rotation stands the **opposite** hitter.

- **Front-row attackers:** three when the setter is in the back row (Rotations 1, 2 and 3: setter in zones 1, 6 and 5), two when the setter is in the front row (Rotations 4, 5 and 6: zones 4, 3 and 2). In the front-row rotations the setter can attack the second ball, which keeps the block honest.
- **Pros:** one setter means one rhythm and one decision-maker. The hitters get the same setter every time. Fewer substitutions are needed.
- **Cons:** the team depends heavily on one player. Half the rotations have only two front-row hitters.
- A typical 5-1 Rotation 1: setter in 1, outside hitter in 2, middle blocker in 3, opposite in 4, outside hitter in 5, middle blocker in 6. Every player has their partner directly opposite: setter and opposite, the two outsides, the two middles.

Most club, college and international teams use a 5-1. The [position explorer](/explorer) shows all six rotations of a 5-1, step by step.

## Comparison

| System | Setters  | Who sets         | Front-row attackers | Good for                      |
| ------ | -------- | ---------------- | ------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| 6-6    | everyone | player in zone 3 | 2                   | beginners, mixed groups       |
| 4-2    | 2        | front-row setter | 2                   | developing teams              |
| 6-2    | 2        | back-row setter  | 3                   | teams with two setter-hitters |
| 5-1    | 1        | the setter       | 3 or 2              | club level and up             |

## Who takes the second ball when the setter cannot?

In every system, the setter sometimes has to dig the first ball. Teams agree in advance who sets instead. Common choices are the libero, the right-side player or whoever is nearest the target. Many teams simply call "**help**" or name the player. Agree on it in training, because the default varies from team to team.

## Choosing a system

- **New team, mixed skill:** start with 6-6, then 4-2.
- **Two players who can set and hit:** 6-2 gives you three attackers without overloading anyone.
- **One clear setter:** 5-1.

Whatever you choose, build the [line-up](/lineup), print the rotation sheets and walk through all six rotations before the first match.
