# Hitter coverage, free balls and down balls

Rotations tell you where to start. These are the moments in between: your team is attacking and must protect its own hitter, or the opponent can only send an easy ball back. Step through them in the [explorer](/explorer): the **Cover** moment after every attack, and **Their ball** in the defense moment.

## Covering your hitter

When your hitter swings into a block, the ball can come straight back down onto your side. Everyone who is not hitting moves in around the hitter, low and still, to keep that ball alive. Coaches call this the cover or the cup.

The pattern most coaching manuals teach is **three close, two deep**: the three players nearest the hitter form a tight half circle around them, about a big step or two away, and the other two cover the deeper court behind them ([Hileno and others, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6006527/); [John Forman, Coaching Volleyball](https://coachingvb.com/hitter-coverage-strategy)). The setter, who has just set, is usually one of the close three.

<Callout>

**Teams vary.** The same study found elite men's teams often use other patterns, with fewer players close. The explorer draws the textbook pattern; follow your coach's spots where they differ.

Tips for covering:

- Get there before the hitter jumps, then stop. Moving while the ball comes off the block makes it impossible to play.
- Stay low, hands ready, eyes on the block rather than the hitter.
- A covered ball does not need to be perfect: send it high so your team can attack again.

## Free ball

A free ball is an easy ball the opponent cannot attack: a pass or a set sent over without a jump. Whoever sees it first calls **“free!”** loudly, and the team changes shape:

- The setter goes straight to the target.
- Front-row hitters drop off the net to behind the 3 m line, so they can pass and still approach.
- Everyone but the setter becomes a passer, usually in a five-player W like serve receive.

This is the standard teaching ([Joel Dearing, _Volleyball Fundamentals_, Human Kinetics](https://us.humankinetics.com/blogs/excerpt/creating-the-w-formation)). USA Volleyball's John Kessel argues against over-drilling it, because in real rallies the free-ball call comes late ([Stop teaching free balls](https://usavolleyball.org/resource/stop-teaching-free-balls/)), but knowing the shape still helps.

## Down ball

A down ball is hit by an opponent who is standing, or without a full jump and swing. It comes over flatter and slower than a spike. On the **“down!”** call, blockers stay at the net but do not jump or raise their hands above the net ([The Art of Coaching Volleyball glossary](https://www.theartofcoachingvolleyball.com/volleyball-glossary/)), and everyone else stays low and deep to dig. Teams position their back row differently here; the explorer keeps it in a deep base.

## When the setter digs the first ball

If the setter plays the first ball, someone else must set. Most teams name a backup:

- **The libero, if on court.** Under FIVB rules, if the libero sets with fingers from in front of the 3 m line, the hitter may not attack that ball while it is entirely above the top of the net ([rule 19.3.1.4](https://www.fivb.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/FIVB-Volleyball_Rules2025_2028-EN-v05.pdf#page=45)). So the libero sets from behind the line, or with a forearm pass.
- **Otherwise the right-front player**, or another front-row player, who then does not attack.

A front-row setter who only touched the ball on the block may still set it: a block touch is not one of the team's three contacts ([FIVB rule 14.4.1](https://www.fivb.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/FIVB-Volleyball_Rules2025_2028-EN-v05.pdf)).

Choose **First ball dug by: The setter** in the explorer's attack moment, when your team serves, to see it.
